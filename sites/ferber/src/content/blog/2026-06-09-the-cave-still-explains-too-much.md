# The Cave Still Explains Too Much

There's a reason the Allegory of the Cave refuses to die. Plato's prisoners watch shadows on a wall and call them reality because the shadows are all they've been given. Turn one of them around, walk him up to the light, and he doesn't thank you. He hurts. Then, if he goes back to tell the others, they think he's lost his mind.

I didn't need a philosophy seminar to recognize that pattern. I needed a few decades of being told what the world was — by churches, by news, by institutions that had a script ready when I asked an inconvenient question — and then a season where the script stopped fitting.

This is not a list of secret societies. It's the working note I keep when something "everybody knows" starts to feel like a shadow.

## Systems Have Authors

Thirty years in IT trains a reflex: when a system behaves oddly, you don't argue with the symptom. You ask who built it, what it optimizes for, and what it hides. DNS that "just works" is somebody's resolver. A "the computer is slow" ticket is a disk, a nic, or a policy. The interface is not the machine.

People are not routers. But narratives are systems. They have authors, incentives, and failure modes. If you would never accept "the vendor said it's fine" as a root-cause analysis, you shouldn't accept it from a pulpit, a feed, or a press conference either.

The cave is just that reflex aimed at meaning instead of packets.

## What I Don't Mean

I don't mean every official story is a lie. I don't mean every dissident is a prophet. Most shadows are boring: habit, fear, the need to belong. Cognitive dissonance, authority bias, and "we've always done it this way" explain more of the wall than any committee in a basement.

I also don't mean you should outsource your map to a Telegram channel. I kept a long reading list once — cave, noble lie, propaganda techniques, mass formation — and the useful part wasn't the list. It was the habit of asking, for each claim: *what's the primary source, who benefits if I believe this, and what would falsify it?*

If a story can't survive those three questions, it's entertainment. Treat it that way.

## The Cost of Turning Around

Leaving a church, a platform, or a consensus doesn't make you free. It makes you responsible for your own epistemology, which is harder. You lose the ready-made community. You lose the relief of letting someone else do the thinking. You pick up the job of testing what you now believe with the same suspicion you used on what you left.

I've written about [stepping outside church walls](/2026-03-31-from-church-walls-to-spiritual-freedom/) and about [reading Scripture like an engineer](/2026-04-01-dispensationalism-makes-sense-to-an-engineer/). Same move, different rooms: stop consuming the approved shadows and go look at the thing itself.

The light isn't a personality. It isn't a feed. It's the discipline of preferring the source over the commentary, even when the commentary is friendlier.

## How I Use This

When something big is being sold as obvious:

1. Find the original document, data, or footage — not the recap.
2. Name the incentive. Who is paid, elected, or followed if this version wins?
3. Write down what would change my mind. If I can't, I'm not investigating. I'm cheering.

That's it. No special knowledge. No inner circle. The cave allegory is 2,400 years old because the wall keeps getting rebuilt with new projectors.

Turn around anyway. The first minutes are worse. The rest of your life is better when the shadows have to earn their place.
